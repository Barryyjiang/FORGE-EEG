How many electrodes does a wearable actually need?

A lab EEG cap has 64–128 electrodes. A consumer wearable has two to eight. This benchmark fixes the number of electrodes and asks what is left: how much accuracy comes from how many electrodes, and how much from where they sit?

The idea in ninety seconds

An EEG cap is a spatial sampler. A wearable is a very small one.

EEG does not read thoughts. It records the summed electrical activity of millions of neurons, blurred by the skull and scalp. The decoder’s job is to find a pattern in that blur that reliably predicts what the person is doing.

1

The brain makes electricity

When neurons fire together, their voltages add up. At the scalp the result is millionths of a volt — tiny, but measurable with a good amplifier.

2

Electrodes sample a mixture

Each electrode sees a blurred combination of many sources. More electrodes give the decoder more views of the same mixture — but placement decides which views it gets.

3

A model learns the pattern

The decoder is trained on labelled trials and scored on held-out ones. The gap between the full cap and a wearable is the price of the channel budget.

Channel count

More channels, more information

Going from four to eight electrodes usually helps. But returns flatten: eight well-placed electrodes recover about 91% of the 22-channel motor-imagery score, and four occipital electrodes recover 98.5% of the SSVEP score.

Channel placement

Where they sit can matter more

In the same motor task, the wrong region is worse than a random subset. Eight in-ear electrodes sit at chance, while eight over motor cortex are statistically indistinguishable from the 115-channel cap.

03 · What it means

Three things the benchmark makes hard to ignore

The headline is not “wearables are bad” or “wearables are fine.” It is that the cost depends on the task, the decoder, and — at small channel counts — very strongly on placement.

4–8 well-placed electrodes

Placement beats count

At four electrodes in motor imagery, the sensorimotor region beats a max-spread subset by +0.036 AUC and random subsets by up to +0.096. Spreading electrodes to maximise coverage is the wrong objective for a focal source.

0.54 → 0.86 P300, two decoders

Decoder choice rivals montage

A generic log-variance decoder sits near chance on P300; xDAWN reaches 0.86. SSVEP sat at 0.13 with band-power and reaches 0.93 with CCA. The “best montage” depends on using the field’s standard decoder.

−0.275 AUC at matched 8 channels

Wearables are task-dependent

Same in-ear site, two tasks: motor execution collapses at matched eight channels, while sleep loses only 0.046 κ at matched six. Broad, slow waves reach the ear; focal sensorimotor rhythms do not.

Accuracy returns quickly — then flattens

Best non-random reduced montage, shown as a percentage of the full-cap score. Four to eight well-placed electrodes recover most of the accuracy in every task where a dense cap exists.

04 · Why you can trust it

What would make these numbers wrong?

A benchmark is only as useful as its failure modes. We run a negative control for every live track, flag small samples and unmatched channel counts, and keep the caveats next to the result rather than in a footnote.

Shuffled labels should land at chance

Each decoder is re-run on permuted labels. If it can still learn, the real result is contaminated — or the control is broken. The controls below are all within tolerance of chance.

Limits we do not hide

These are the reasons a number here may not transfer to a real product.

  • Tier 1, not a wearable simulator. Deleting channels from a dense cap ignores the reference, volume conduction, motion artifact and the wearable’s noise floor.
  • Proxy montages are geometry only. “cEEGrid proxy” and “earbud proxy” are nearest 10-5 rings, not measurements of those products.
  • Small samples. Motor execution has 6 subjects, sleep 5 nights, c-VEP 1 subject. At n=6, p=0.031 means “all six subjects agree in sign,” not “highly significant.”
  • The ear result contradicts a published paper. Wu et al. report above-chance in-ear motor decoding with methods designed for it. Our generic motor-cortex pipeline is not that method, so “ear is at chance” is partly a decoder limitation.
  • Model capacity is not held constant. A 4-channel product would not ship a 64-channel model. Part of the low-channel drop is fewer electrodes; part is less overfitting.
  • c-VEP uses the wrong decoder. 0.400 is a lower bound. Template matching or CCA against the known codes is the standard method.

05 · Methods & reproduction

One data change, one decoder, one report

The benchmark is a declarative cross-product of dataset, paradigm, protocol, montage and decoder. A track is one (dataset, paradigm, protocol) entry; a decoder is one registry entry. The runner and leaderboard pick both up automatically.

How a track is evaluated

1 · dataPublic dataset
2 · montageEqual-N subsets
3 · decoderFold-wise fit
4 · reportΔ vs full cap
  • Equal-N: every geometric condition has exactly k channels; random seeds are the chance control.
  • Nested spread: farthest-point order gives a prefix of length k for a monotone accuracy-vs-k curve.
  • Paired deltas: comparisons pair on subject-session, so between-subject variance cancels.
  • Chance is per row: ROC-AUC 0.5, accuracy 1/n, Cohen’s κ 0.
  • Resumable: one CSV per track × protocol; re-running skips completed files.

Reproduce the page and the numbers

The site reads results/benchmark/*.csv and writes a single JSON bundle. No result is typed into the HTML by hand.

# rebuild the benchmark tables
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe scripts\report.py

# rebuild the site data (numbers)
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe scripts\build_site.py

# optional: real EEG illustrations
.\.venv\Scripts\python.exe scripts\build_site_signals.py

# preview locally
python -m http.server 8000 --directory docs

What this is not

  • A wearable product evaluation. Deleting channels from a dense cap is not a forward model of a wearable.
  • A claim that ear-EEG is uninformative. It is a claim about this pipeline, this task and this montage.
  • A leaderboard of deep models. EEGNet failed its own scalp control and is excluded until it is stable.
  • A substitute for the published papers. Every dataset has a canonical analysis; this benchmark asks a different, narrower question.
